Project Context & Background

Why This Project Existed

Modern smartphones have grown to 6.1"–6.9" displays, yet the dominant UI paradigm still places primary navigation and actions in the top 30% of the screen — the hardest area to reach with one hand. This research project explored how mobile interfaces can be redesigned around natural thumb ergonomics, using bottom-anchored interaction patterns, gesture-driven navigation, and spring physics animations that provide tactile feedback in a digital medium.

The Problem Statement

Friction & Roadblocks Faced by Users

Three critical problems with current mobile UX: (1) 'Thumb gymnastics' — users contort their hands to reach top-placed navigation bars, increasing device drop risk and causing hand fatigue during extended use. (2) 'Tap ambiguity' — small touch targets in dense UIs cause frequent mis-taps, especially in motion (walking, transit). (3) 'Dead animation' — most UI transitions use linear easing that feels robotic and disconnected from physical reality. Users subconsciously expect digital objects to behave with the physics of real objects (momentum, spring, friction).

Research & Discovery

How We Listened to the User

▸Thumb-Reach Heatmap Study: Mapped the natural thumb reach zones on 6.1" (iPhone 14) and 6.7" (iPhone 14 Plus) displays. Created a heat map categorizing screen areas as Easy (bottom 40%), Stretch (middle 30%), and Hard (top 30%). Found that 72% of smartphone users operate one-handed in real contexts.
▸Tap Target Size Testing: Conducted a rapid-fire tap accuracy test with 10 participants using targets ranging from 32px to 64px. Results: 32px targets had a 23% miss rate, 44px targets had 8%, and 52px+ targets had <2% miss rate. Sweaty conditions (post-exercise) increased miss rates by 40%.
▸Animation Perception Study: Showed participants 3 versions of the same UI transition: linear easing, ease-in-out, and spring physics (damping: 0.7, stiffness: 300). Spring physics consistently rated highest for 'feeling natural' and 'responsive.' Participants described linear easing as 'robotic' and 'laggy' even when objectively faster.
▸Competitive Gesture Analysis: Deconstructed gesture interaction patterns from Apple Maps (bottom sheet), Spotify (swipe-to-queue), and Telegram (long-press reactions). Identified 8 gesture patterns that users already understand intuitively.
Step-by-Step Architecture

The Design & Iteration Process

Ergonomic Research

Sprint 1

Built thumb-reach heatmaps for 3 device sizes (5.4", 6.1", 6.7"). Identified the 'comfort zone' — bottom 40% of screen where primary actions should live. Defined minimum tap target sizes for different contexts: 44px (stationary), 48px (walking), 52px (sweaty/active).

Gesture Library Design

Sprint 2

Designed a gesture vocabulary of 8 interactions: tap, long-press, swipe-horizontal, swipe-vertical, pull-down-to-refresh, bottom-sheet-drag, pinch-zoom, and double-tap-zoom. Each gesture has a defined trigger zone, haptic feedback pattern, and reversibility mechanism.

Spring Physics Animation

Sprint 3

Built all transitions using spring-based easing curves in Figma Smart Animate. Tuned damping (0.65–0.8) and stiffness (200–400) values to match iOS native feel. Created overshoot animations for bottom sheet snapping that provide satisfying 'click into place' feedback.

Interactive Prototype

Sprint 4

Assembled the complete prototype with 40+ interactive states. Every screen demonstrates bottom-anchored navigation, gesture-driven content browsing, and spring-animated transitions. Added micro-interactions: button press states (scale 0.95 + shadow reduction), loading shimmer effects, and success state celebrations.

Architectural Solution

Engineered for Usability & Precision

Engineered a bottom-sheet driven interaction prototype using Figma Smart Animate with realistic spring physics, tactile confirmation feedback, and one-thumb touch targets. Every primary action is placed within the natural thumb comfort zone, gestures have clear affordances and reversibility, and transitions use spring physics that match iOS native motion.

Measured Outcome & Business Value

Quantitative & Qualitative Results

Recorded zero tap misses during usability testing with 10 participants and 100% flow completion speed in single-hand mobile usage. Participants consistently described the prototype as 'feeling like a native app' despite being a Figma prototype. The spring physics animation library has been reused across 3 subsequent mobile projects.

Retrospective & Honest Takeaways

What I Learned & What I'd Do Next

This project reinforced that mobile design is fundamentally an ergonomic discipline, not just a visual one. The most polished UI is useless if users can't comfortably reach its buttons. The spring physics research was particularly eye-opening — the difference between linear and spring-based animation is the difference between a UI feeling 'digital' and feeling 'physical.' I now apply spring easing as a default across all mobile projects, and the bottom-anchored navigation pattern has become a core principle in my mobile design philosophy.
