Project Context & Background

Why This Project Existed

India's agricultural sector serves over 150 million farming households, yet digital penetration in agricultural supply chains remains below 5%. Farmers and local distributors continue to rely on physical markets (mandis) and word-of-mouth recommendations for purchasing fertilizers, seeds, and pesticides — resulting in price exploitation, counterfeit products, and supply chain inefficiencies. This project aimed to create an accessible mobile e-commerce platform that could serve rural farmers with limited digital literacy and low-bandwidth internet access.

The Problem Statement

Friction & Roadblocks Faced by Users

Five unique challenges differentiated this from typical e-commerce design: (1) Low digital literacy — many target users had never used a shopping app before and navigated primarily through visual recognition, not text. (2) Low-bandwidth environments — 2G/3G connections in rural areas meant heavy images and complex animations were impractical. (3) Complex product naming — fertilizer types like 'Di-Ammonium Phosphate (DAP)' and 'Muriate of Potash (MOP)' are confusing even for experienced farmers. (4) Seasonal purchasing patterns — farmers buy in seasonal cycles aligned with crop calendars, not impulse buying. (5) Trust deficit — farmers are wary of online purchases due to counterfeit product experiences.

Research & Discovery

How We Listened to the User

▸Field Research (In-Person): Visited 3 agricultural markets (mandis) in rural Gujarat. Observed how farmers select and purchase fertilizers in physical stores — they rely heavily on packaging color recognition, brand logos, and shopkeeper recommendations rather than reading product labels.
▸Farmer Persona Development: Built 3 detailed personas through interviews with 15 farmers: - 'Ramesh' (Small-holder farmer, 2 acres, budget-conscious, feature phone user upgrading to smartphone) - 'Priya' (Progressive farmer, 8 acres, uses YouTube for farming tips, comfortable with apps) - 'Bharat' (Distributor, supplies to 50+ farmers, needs bulk ordering and credit terms)
▸Digital Literacy Assessment: Tested basic smartphone interactions (tap, scroll, swipe, type) with 8 participants. Found that typing was the biggest barrier — search-by-text was nearly unusable. Voice search and visual browsing (category icons + product images) were strongly preferred.
▸Network Performance Profiling: Tested app loading on actual 2G/3G connections in rural areas. Established performance budgets: pages must load within 3 seconds on 3G, images must be under 50KB each, and the app must function with intermittent connectivity (offline cart saving).
Step-by-Step Architecture

The Design & Iteration Process

Field Ethnography

Week 1

Spent 3 days in rural agricultural markets observing purchasing behaviors. Documented the physical shopping journey from market entry → browsing → shopkeeper consultation → price negotiation → purchase. Identified that farmers trust visual packaging and brand colors more than product names.

Persona & Journey Mapping

Week 1–2

Created 3 user personas (Ramesh, Priya, Bharat) with detailed context scenarios. Mapped end-to-end purchasing journeys for each, identifying 7 friction points in the digital equivalent: search, product identification, dosage/quantity selection, cart review, payment, delivery tracking, and reorder.

Paper Prototyping

Week 2

Before going digital, created paper prototypes and tested with 5 farmers at a local chai stall. This was essential — farmers who had never used an app could point at paper screens and say 'I would tap here.' Discovered that bottom-tab navigation was intuitive, but hamburger menus were completely invisible to first-time app users.

Visual Design for Accessibility

Week 2–3

Designed with extreme accessibility constraints: minimum 16px body text, maximum 3 navigation levels deep, product cards with large images (60% of card area) and minimal text, high-contrast color palette meeting WCAG AA for outdoor sunlight readability, and prominently displayed price in large bold numerals (the first thing farmers look for).

Performance Optimization

Week 3–4

Designed all screens with a 50KB image budget per product card. Used progressive image loading (blurred placeholder → full image). Designed an offline-capable cart that syncs when connectivity returns. Created a 1-tap reorder flow for repeat purchases — farmers buy the same products every season, so reducing repeat purchase friction was critical.

Architectural Solution

Engineered for Usability & Precision

Designed an accessible mobile e-commerce application featuring high-contrast typography, large tactile buttons (minimum 56×56px), localized iconography, visual packaging previews matching physical product appearance, a 1-tap reorder flow for seasonal repeat purchases, and progressive image loading optimized for low-bandwidth rural networks.

Measured Outcome & Business Value

Quantitative & Qualitative Results

Validated through field testing with 8 rural users, reducing checkout completion time by 52% compared to the benchmark agricultural app. Zero users required assistance to complete a purchase after the paper prototype iteration. The 1-tap reorder feature was identified as the most valued functionality, with every test participant saying they would 'definitely use it.' Order errors (wrong product, wrong quantity) dropped to zero in final testing.

Retrospective & Honest Takeaways

What I Learned & What I'd Do Next

This was the most humbling project of my career. Designing for users with limited digital literacy stripped away every UX convention I took for granted — hamburger menus, text-based search, complex gestures, multi-step forms. It forced me to think about design from first principles: 'If someone has never tapped a button on a screen before, what should that button look like?' Paper prototyping in the field, testing at a chai stall, watching a farmer's face light up when they found 'their' fertilizer by its packaging photo — that's design research at its most raw and valuable. This project shaped my core belief: 'It doesn't matter what you & I think. It only matters what our user thinks.'
