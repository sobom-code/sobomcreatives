Project Context & Background

Why This Project Existed

As a design exercise and industry contribution, I conducted unsolicited UX audits and redesigns of well-known consumer products — including Swiggy (India's leading food delivery app), Lastcard (fintech), and Rocketline (SaaS landing page) — plus sign-up flow redesigns for Naavigo, mio.io, and Social Tribe. These redesigns were published as detailed case studies on my WordPress blog, demonstrating systematic UX evaluation methodology and providing actionable improvement recommendations.

The Problem Statement

Friction & Roadblocks Faced by Users

Each product had unique UX friction points, but common patterns emerged: (1) Swiggy's cart/order review screen was visually noisy with competing CTAs, promotional banners, and dense item lists — users couldn't quickly verify their order. (2) Lastcard's hero section failed to communicate the product's value proposition within 5 seconds, burying the core message below the fold. (3) Rocketline's landing page had poor visual hierarchy with equal-weight sections that competed for attention. (4) Multiple sign-up flows (Naavigo, mio.io, Social Tribe) created unnecessary friction through excessive form fields and unclear progress indicators.

Research & Discovery

How We Listened to the User

▸Nielsen's 10 Heuristic Evaluation: Applied Jakob Nielsen's usability heuristics systematically to each product. Scored every screen on a 0–4 severity scale across all 10 heuristics. Created detailed violation reports with specific UI elements cited.
▸Visual Hierarchy Analysis: Used squint tests and 5-second tests to evaluate information hierarchy. The squint test reveals whether the most important elements are visually dominant when details are blurred. The 5-second test measures what users remember after a 5-second exposure to a screen.
▸Cognitive Walkthrough: Performed task-based cognitive walkthroughs for each product's primary user flow. For Swiggy: 'Add items to cart → Review order → Apply coupon → Place order.' Identified 6 cognitive friction points where users had to pause, think, or re-read.
▸Attention Heatmap Estimation: Based on F-pattern and Z-pattern reading research, predicted user attention distribution on each screen and identified elements placed in low-attention zones despite being critical (e.g., Lastcard's value proposition headline was in a low-attention zone on mobile).
▸Before/After Validation: Presented redesigns to 12 designers and product managers for peer review. Collected structured feedback on clarity improvement, visual hierarchy, and actionability of changes.
Step-by-Step Architecture

The Design & Iteration Process

Heuristic Audit

Per Product: Day 1–2

Applied all 10 Nielsen heuristics to each product's key screens. Documented every violation with severity rating (1=cosmetic, 2=minor, 3=major, 4=catastrophe), specific UI element screenshot, heuristic violated, and recommended fix. Swiggy had 14 violations (3 major), Lastcard had 9 (2 major), Rocketline had 11 (1 catastrophe-level).

Information Prioritization

Per Product: Day 2–3

For each screen, ranked all UI elements by user importance (what do they NEED to see?) vs. business importance (what does the company WANT them to see?). Identified misalignments — e.g., Swiggy's cart page gave more visual weight to promotional banners than the order summary itself.

Tactical Redesign

Per Product: Day 3–5

Executed focused redesigns that addressed the highest-severity violations while respecting each product's existing brand system. No wholesale redesigns — only targeted improvements that could be implemented without brand approval. This constraint made the recommendations immediately actionable for product teams.

Before/After Documentation

Per Product: Day 5–6

Created detailed side-by-side before/after comparisons with annotation callouts explaining every change and the heuristic or research principle behind it. Published as long-form articles on WordPress with embedded Figma prototypes.

Architectural Solution

Engineered for Usability & Precision

Executed tactical redesigns: overhauled Swiggy's cart order review into a glanceable single-card format with clear item grouping and prominent total, redesigned Lastcard's hero section for immediate value clarity with a benefit-first headline, optimized Rocketline's responsive layout with clear visual hierarchy, and streamlined 3 sign-up flows (Naavigo, mio.io, Social Tribe) by reducing form fields and adding progress indicators.

Measured Outcome & Business Value

Quantitative & Qualitative Results

Retrospective & Honest Takeaways

What I Learned & What I'd Do Next

Unsolicited redesigns are controversial in the design community — some argue they're disrespectful to the original team's constraints. I approach them differently: as systematic UX evaluation exercises that benefit both the redesigner (deepening heuristic analysis skills) and the community (providing actionable examples of UX improvement). The key is to redesign with humility — acknowledging that the original team had constraints you don't know about, and framing your work as 'here's what I'd explore' rather than 'here's what's wrong.' The heuristic framework I refined during this series now saves me 40% of audit time on client projects because the evaluation checklist and severity taxonomy are pre-built.
